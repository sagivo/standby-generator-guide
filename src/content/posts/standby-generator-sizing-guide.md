Undersizing is the most expensive generator mistake: the unit trips the moment your AC compressor kicks in, and you're back in the dark with a $12,000 paperweight. Here's how to get the number right.

[[diagram:sizing]]

## The method in 4 steps

**1. List what stays on.** Fridge, freezer, furnace fan or heat, lights, internet, sump pump, well pump if you have one, and air conditioning. Be honest — "everything" is a valid answer, it just costs more.

**2. Add running watts.** Typical figures: fridge 200 W, furnace fan 800 W, sump pump 800 W, well pump 1,000 W, electric dryer 5,000 W, electric range 8,000 W, EV charger 7,200 W. AC by tonnage: 2-ton 2,500 W, 3-ton 3,500 W, 4-ton 5,000 W, 5-ton 6,000 W.

**3. Add the biggest starting surge.** Motors surge when they start — a 3-ton AC needs ~12,000 W for a few seconds. Take the single largest (surge − running) and add it once. You don't stack surges; they don't all start at the same instant.

**4. Add margin and round up.** Add 20–25%, then round up to the next standard size: 14, 18, 20, 22, 24, or 26 kW.

## The fuel derate trap

This is where buyers get burned. Several popular models are rated at full kW on propane but derate ~2 kW on natural gas — the Kohler 20RESCL is 20 kW on LP but 18 kW on NG; the Champion 14kW is 14 on LP, 12.5 on NG. If you're on natural gas, do the entire calculation against the NG rating.

## Rules of thumb (then verify)

- **14 kW:** essentials + one small AC, up to ~1,800 sq ft, with load management.
- **18–20 kW:** most of a 1,500–2,500 sq ft home, one central AC zone.
- **22–26 kW:** true whole-house, 2,500+ sq ft, multiple AC zones.
