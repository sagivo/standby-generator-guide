Every brand advertises a "5-year warranty," and they are not the same warranty. One covers labor for two years, another for five, another runs seven years with no step-down at all — and the hour cap buried in the fine print can matter more than the year count. If you're comparing units (see [Generac vs Kohler](/posts/generac-vs-kohler/) for the full brand breakdown), here's what the warranties actually promise, and where they quietly end.

## At a glance

| | Generac Guardian | Kohler RCA/RES | Cummins QuietConnect | Briggs & Stratton PowerProtect+ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Term | 5 years / 2,000 hours | 5 years / 2,000 hours | 5 years / 2,000 hours | **7 years** comprehensive |
| Parts | Full in yrs 1–3, major components in yrs 4–5 | Full, all 5 years | Full, all 5 years | Full, all 7 years |
| Labor | Yrs 1–2 only | Full, all 5 years | Full, all 5 years | Full, all 7 years |
| Travel | Yrs 1–2, limited | Full, all 5 years | Per terms | Full, all 7 years |
| Extended available | Yes, through dealers | Yes — to 10 years | Yes — to 10 years | Yes — 10-year dealer-exclusive |

(Details below are from the brands' published warranty documents and dealer references. Terms change; always confirm against the warranty card that ships with your unit.)

## Generac: 5 years, but coverage steps down

Generac's published residential standby warranty is **5 years or 2,000 hours, whichever comes first**, and the coverage level shrinks as the years go by:

- **Years 1–2:** parts, labor, and limited travel. This is the period when most failures actually happen — controllers, batteries, sensors — so the strongest coverage lands where it matters.
- **Year 3:** parts only. You buy the labor.
- **Years 4–5:** major components only — specifically the engine short block and the alternator rotor and stator. A failed controller or transfer-switch part in year 4 is on your dime.

The warranty begins on **successful startup/activation** (not on the purchase date), the unit should be registered with proof of purchase available, and scheduled maintenance per the owner's manual is required — performed by an authorized dealer, though owner-performed maintenance with genuine parts and receipts is standard practice. It is **transferable** between owners at the original installation site, which is a nice detail when you sell. Exclusions include non-Generac replacement parts and emissions components (those carry a separate emissions warranty). Extended warranties are available, but only through Generac dealers.

The practical takeaway: Generac's warranty is strongest exactly when the unit is most likely to have teething problems, and thinnest exactly when expensive components start aging. Price the extended warranty accordingly.

## Kohler: the quiet best value in the fine print

Kohler's published term is also **5 years / 2,000 hours** — but the coverage doesn't step down. Parts, labor, **and travel** are covered for the entire term. That travel coverage is the sleeper detail: a dealer service call in year 4 on a Generac means you pay labor and travel; on a Kohler it's covered.

Two things to know: Kohler's warranty for **off-grid applications** drops to 18 months / 1,000 hours (it also shortens for commercial-duty use in some lines), so if the generator will be a primary power source rather than standby, the math changes. And Kohler extends to **10 years through dealers**, which is one of the longest-backed horizons in residential standby.

Why it matters: in our [reliability comparison](/posts/generac-vs-kohler-reliability/), Kohler showed fewer failures per installed unit — but failures still happen, and a flat coverage curve means fewer surprise bills in years 3–5.

## Cummins: 5 years with room to grow

Cummins' QuietConnect line carries a **base warranty of 5 years / 2,000 hours** with extended warranties available — dealer references put the extension at up to 10 years. Cummins leans on its commercial-power heritage (the company also builds the industrial gensets that hospitals run), and the warranty structure reads like a commercial policy scaled down for homes: straightforward term, extensions through the dealer channel.

## Briggs & Stratton: the 7-year outlier

The current **PowerProtect+ lineup** carries an industry-leading **7-year comprehensive standard warranty covering parts, labor, and travel for the full seven years** — no step-down in years 3 or 5, which is what makes it genuinely different from Generac's tiered structure. Briggs & Stratton states it explicitly in its marketing: unlike competitor warranties that reduce coverage over time, you get the full term. For units purchased from a Briggs & Stratton dealer, a **10-year dealer-exclusive warranty** is also available.

One caveat: older Briggs & Stratton models (e.g., the 12kW series sold a few years back) carried a 5-year parts/labor/travel term, so if you're buying used or from old dealer stock, verify which warranty document ships with the serial number you're getting. The 7-year term applies to the current PowerProtect+ lineup.

## The hour cap nobody reads

"5 years or 2,000 hours, whichever comes first" sounds generous until you do the arithmetic. Exercise cycles burn roughly 40 hours a year, so under normal standby duty the hour cap never binds — 2,000 hours would take ~45 years of exercise alone. But a long outage changes the math: a week-long ice-storm outage at 100+ hours is real, and a handful of those over five years starts to matter. Nobody commonly exhausts 2,000 hours in five years of residential standby, but if your feeder goes down for days every storm season, the hour cap is the term to watch — not the year count.

## What actually voids or shrinks coverage

Across all four brands, the denial triggers are the same:

1. **Non-genuine parts.** All four warranties exclude failures caused by third-party parts. Use OEM oil filters, air filters, and spark plugs (or genuine-equivalent kits from a dealer) and keep the receipts.
2. **Missed scheduled maintenance.** The owner's manual schedule isn't a suggestion for warranty purposes. Our [maintenance checklist](/posts/standby-generator-maintenance/) covers what's due and when — keep a log.
3. **Wrong duty.** Running a standby-rated unit as primary/off-grid power voids or shortens coverage (Kohler's off-grid 18-month term is the explicit version; all brands limit to "standby" duty as defined in the document).
4. **Unregistered unit / no proof of purchase.** Register the unit after installation. It takes five minutes and some brands tie warranty start to it.
5. **Improper installation.** DIY transfer-switch wiring or an unqualified installer can void coverage on electrical faults. This is part of why [professional installation](/posts/standby-generator-installation-cost/) isn't just about the labor — it's about the warranty standing behind it.

## Are extended warranties worth it?

Honest answer: they're insurance on the most failure-prone years. The bathtub curve is real — failures cluster early (manufacturing defects, caught by the strong year 1–2 coverage) and late (wear-out: alternators, engine internals in years 6–10). An extended warranty that pushes coverage to 10 years is buying exactly the wear-out tail. Whether it's worth it depends on the price: as a rule of thumb, compare the extension cost against setting the same money aside in a repair reserve. If the dealer wants $800 for two extra years, the reserve often wins. If it's a few hundred folded into the install package, take it — especially on a Generac, where the standard warranty is thinnest in years 3–5.

## The verdict

If warranty length is your deciding factor, **Briggs & Stratton's 7-year comprehensive term is the clear leader** — it covers what others exclude in years 3–5 and 6–7. If you're choosing between Generac and Kohler, **Kohler's flat parts/labor/travel coverage for all five years is the better fine print**, even though both say "5-year warranty" on the box. And regardless of brand: register the unit, follow the maintenance schedule, use genuine parts, and keep the receipts. The best warranty in the industry is worthless without the paper trail to claim it.
