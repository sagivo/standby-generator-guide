"How much will it cost to run?" is the question buyers ask after the sticker shock fades — and it's the right question, because a generator that runs for a week straight on propane can burn through hundreds of dollars in fuel. Here are real numbers from manufacturer spec sheets, plus the math to plan your outage.

## The headline figures (20 kW class)

These are published spec-sheet figures at half and full load — the two numbers that bracket real-world use:

| Model | NG half load | NG full load | LP half load | LP full load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cummins RS20A (20 kW) | 172 cu-ft/hr | 268 cu-ft/hr | 1.8 gal/hr | 3.1 gal/hr |
| Kohler 20RESCL (20/18 kW) | 161 cu-ft/hr | 281 cu-ft/hr | 2.2 gal/hr | 3.7 gal/hr |
| Briggs & Stratton 20kW (40584) | 187 cu-ft/hr | 260 cu-ft/hr | 2.06 gal/hr | 3.28 gal/hr |
| Generac 24kW (7209/7210) | — | 203 cu-ft/hr | — | — |
| Generac 22kW (7043) | — | 228 cu-ft/hr | — | — |

Two things jump out. First, the Generac 24kW is the efficiency standout — 203 cu-ft/hr at full load beats the 22kW's 228 despite making more power. Second, the spread between brands at the same load is modest: at half load on propane, every 20 kW-class unit here burns roughly 2 gallons an hour. Don't pick a brand on fuel economy alone; pick on features, dealer network, and price.

## What it costs per hour

**Natural gas.** Residential NG is commonly ~$1.00–$1.80 per therm (100 cu-ft), varying wildly by region and season. At 200 cu-ft/hr (a 20kW at moderate load), that's roughly **$2–$4/hour**. A 3-day outage (72 hours) at moderate load: ~$150–$290 in gas. The meter keeps spinning, but there's no tank to watch and no delivery to schedule.

**Propane.** Residential propane is commonly ~$2.50–$4.00/gallon depending on region, season, and whether you own or lease the tank. At 2.2 gal/hr (half load), that's roughly **$5.50–$9/hour**. The same 3-day outage: ~$400–$650 in propane. Propane's cost sting is real — but so is its independence from utility infrastructure.

These are planning numbers, not quotes: your utility rate, your propane contract, and your actual load all move them. But the ratio holds just about everywhere — propane costs roughly 2× natural gas per hour of the same work.

## How long your propane lasts (the tank math)

Propane tanks are filled to 80% for expansion safety, so "500 gallons" means ~400 usable gallons. At typical half-load burn (~2–2.5 gal/hr on a 20 kW unit):

| Tank size | Usable gallons | Half-load runtime | Full-load runtime |
|---|---|---|---|
| 250 gal | ~200 | ~3–4 days | ~2–3 days |
| 500 gal | ~400 | ~6–8 days | ~4–5 days |
| 1,000 gal | ~800 | ~12–16 days | ~8–10 days |

This is why tank sizing is part of generator sizing. If your region's worst outages run 2–3 days (most of suburban America), a 500-gallon tank is generous. If you're the last house on a rural feeder that goes down for a week every ice storm, the 1,000-gallon tank is cheap insurance — and some buyers add a second 500 rather than one 1,000 for delivery flexibility.

One more propane note: in a widespread outage, **delivery trucks may not reach you**. Size the tank for the outage, not for the average — the fuel you have on day one is the fuel you have.

## Natural gas: the meter question

Natural gas buyers skip the tank math, but there's a different check: **can your gas meter supply the generator plus the house?** A 20 kW unit at full load wants ~280 cu-ft/hr; add a furnace (~100–150), water heater (~40), and range, and many standard residential meters are undersized. Gas utilities handle meter upsizes routinely — often free or cheap — but it must be arranged **before** the install, not discovered during it. Ask your installer to confirm meter capacity with the utility as part of the quote. Our [installation cost guide](/posts/standby-generator-installation-cost/) covers this.

Also note the pressure angle: NG generators want 5–7 inches of water column at the unit. Long gas runs with too-small pipe cause pressure drop, which causes rough running and fault codes. This is plumbing, not a generator defect — size the gas line for the distance.

## Does a bigger generator drink more?

Less than you'd think. Fuel consumption follows **electrical load**, not nameplate capacity. A 26 kW unit carrying 10 kW of house burns about the same as a 20 kW carrying the same 10 kW — the engine just loafs more. The differences show up at the margins:

- At very light loads, bigger engines are slightly less efficient (more internal friction per kWh).
- At high loads, newer/larger designs can be **more** efficient — the Generac 24kW beating the 22kW's full-load NG burn is the proof.
- The weekly exercise cycle burns a trivial amount either way (~0.5–1 hour equivalent per week).

Don't downsize to save fuel. Size for the loads ([kW calculator](/tools/kw-size-calculator/)), then let the load decide the burn.

## Cutting fuel costs during an outage

- **Manage big loads.** The dryer, the EV charger, and the second AC zone are optional during an outage. Every kW you shed is fuel you don't burn — roughly $0.15–$0.40/hour per kW on NG, $0.30–$0.60 on propane.
- **Use load-shed modules.** They automate this: the water heater and other deferrable loads drop out when the generator nears capacity, then come back. Less fuel, less babysitting.
- **Keep up with maintenance.** A fouled air filter or old spark plugs measurably hurt efficiency. The [maintenance checklist](/posts/standby-generator-maintenance/) is an hour a year.
- **On propane, pre-buy.** Fill the tank in summer when prices dip, and top off before storm season. A full tank in September is the cheapest outage insurance there is.

## The exercise cycle: the fuel you burn when nothing's wrong

Don't forget the quiet consumer: the weekly exercise cycle. A 5–12 minute self-test every week adds up to roughly 4–10 hours of runtime a year — at light load, that's on the order of 10–25 gallons of propane or 800–2,000 cu-ft of natural gas annually. In dollar terms: **$25–$75/year on propane, $10–$30/year on natural gas.** Negligible next to the maintenance budget, but it's the reason your propane gauge moves even in a year with zero outages. If the tank level puzzles you in October, the exercise cycle is usually the answer.
