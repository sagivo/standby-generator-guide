Generac sells more home standby generators than every other brand combined, and the Guardian 22kW is the default quote on most installers' lips. Briggs & Stratton, meanwhile, built the commercial engines that live in millions of mowers and industrial machines — and its PowerProtect line backs that pedigree with a 10-year limited parts warranty that embarrasses the rest of the industry.

So which do you buy? This is one of the cleaner matchups in standby: the spec sheets are close, the prices are close, and the decision comes down to two genuinely different things — **warranty length versus dealer-network scale**. Everything below uses the two flagships of the fight: the Briggs & Stratton PowerProtect 20kW and the Generac Guardian 22kW.

## Engines: Vanguard vs G-Force

Engines are where each brand stakes its identity. Briggs & Stratton's Vanguard line is its commercial engine division — the engines in commercial zero-turns, pumps, and equipment that run thousands of hours a year. Putting a commercial-grade engine in a machine that mostly idles through weekly exercise cycles is arguably overbuilt, and that's the pitch: headroom you never use is exactly what longevity is made of.

Generac's G-Force engines, by contrast, were designed *for* standby generators from the start. The 1000 Series V-twin in the 22kW is purpose-built for standby duty — optimized for the constant 3,600 RPM single-speed grind, low oil consumption over long runs, and the 20-minute weekly exercise routine. It doesn't have the Vanguard's commercial-mower pedigree, but it has something arguably more valuable in a standby context: it is the highest-volume standby engine in the world, with a failure-history database measured in the millions of units.

Both are overhead-valve V-twins with cast-iron cylinder liners in this class. Neither is liquid-cooled. The engineering philosophies differ — commercial pedigree vs. purpose-built scale — but for the buyer's purposes, they're both proven designs. The engine won't be what decides this matchup; the warranty and the network will.

## Warranty: Briggs' biggest weapon

This is the sharpest contrast on either spec sheet. Briggs & Stratton's PowerProtect line carries a **10-year limited parts warranty** — double the 5-year standard that Generac, Kohler, and Cummins all share. The full [standby generator warranty comparison](/posts/standby-generator-warranty-comparison/) breaks down what "limited parts" covers and excludes, but the headline is real: ten years of parts coverage is the longest standard warranty in residential standby.

Generac's 5-year limited warranty is standard for the industry, with one quiet advantage worth knowing: **no hour cap**. In long-outage regions, an outage that runs hundreds of hours won't push you past a warranty hour limit, because there isn't one. Kohler and Cummins cap theirs at 2,000 hours; Generac simply doesn't.

Which matters more depends on your time horizon. If you plan to own the home for a decade-plus and want the longest paper promise, Briggs wins outright. If you value the industry-standard coverage plus the deepest service network to actually honor it, Generac's five years look more useful than five years from a brand with a thinner dealer bench. A warranty is only as good as the technician who honors it — which brings us to the real tiebreaker.

## The transfer switch and load management

Generac bundles the **200A service-entrance-rated automatic transfer switch** in every Guardian kit, and its load-shedding ecosystem — Smart Management Modules on individual circuits — is the most deployed in the industry. Your installer has wired it dozens of times.

Briggs & Stratton's kits are less uniform. Bundled PowerProtect kits include a 200A transfer switch, but bare-unit listings are common on marketplace sites, and the ATS-included vs. bare-unit difference swings project cost by over a thousand dollars. Confirm the exact kit contents before ordering — the Amazon listing title doesn't always match the box. Briggs' load-management answer is its **Symphony II** power-management system on select kits, which prioritizes high-demand circuits the same way Generac's shedding modules do.

Neither brand's transfer technology is a differentiator here; both switch in under a minute (typically ~10–30 seconds after utility loss, following the sensing and warmup sequence). The differentiator is packaging clarity: Generac's "everything in the box" approach versus Briggs' "check the SKU" reality. Read our [automatic transfer switch explained](/posts/automatic-transfer-switch-explained/) guide if you want to understand what's actually in that box.

## Monitoring: InfoHub vs Mobile Link

Remote monitoring matters more than buyers expect — especially for second homes or frequent travelers, where a generator that silently fails its exercise cycles is worse than no generator at all. Both brands include it free, which wasn't true five years ago.

**Briggs InfoHub** is wireless monitoring with **no subscription fee**: status, maintenance reminders, and alerts to your phone. **Generac Mobile Link** (Wi-Fi on current Guardians, cellular optional) offers similar coverage with deeper diagnostic detail and one of the largest installed monitoring fleets in the industry — which means the failure-pattern detection benefits from more data.

Practically: both tell you the unit ran its exercise, both alert on faults, both are free. Generac's edge is ecosystem depth; Briggs' is simplicity. Have the installer connect the monitoring to your network during commissioning and verify the app shows the unit before the truck leaves — regardless of brand.

## The dealer network: Generac's real moat

Here's the uncomfortable truth of the matchup. In a perfect world, the Briggs wins: equal-ish specs, longer warranty, similar price. But standby generators are installed and serviced by humans, and Generac has more of them — the largest certified dealer network in residential standby, in virtually every metro in the country.

Why that matters:

- **Quotes are competitive.** Three Generac-certified installers in your ZIP will sharpen each other's pencils. A lone Briggs servicer has no incentive to.
- **Parts are local.** A failed controller board on a Saturday is a two-day wait with a Generac and potentially a two-week wait with a brand whose distributor is three states away.
- **Experience depth.** The Guardian 22kW is the unit most technicians have installed dozens of times. Familiarity prevents the miswired ATS, the undersized gas line, the bad pad.

The Briggs & Stratton review puts it bluntly: confirm there's a servicer within reasonable distance *before* you buy. If there is — a solid local Briggs dealer with good reviews — the warranty advantage is genuinely worth capturing. If there isn't, the 10-year warranty is a promise from someone far away.

## Fuel and noise

Fuel consumption between the two is a wash — the spec-sheet figures at the same loads are within 10–20% of each other, as the [standby generator fuel consumption](/posts/standby-generator-fuel-consumption/) breakdown documents. At half load on propane, both burn roughly 2 gallons an hour. Don't pick a brand on fuel economy; pick on features, network, and price.

Noise is similarly close. Generac's Quiet-Test runs the weekly self-exercise at a lower RPM (~67 dB(A) for the 22kW) so the machine doesn't announce itself every Sunday morning. Briggs publishes noise figures in the same typical air-cooled band for the PowerProtect line. Neither is a whisper-quiet unit — for that tier, the [quiet standby generators](/posts/best-quiet-standby-generators/) roundup covers the brands that market low decibels hardest (Kohler's aluminum-enclosure line and Cummins' QuietConnect).

## Who should buy which

**Buy the Briggs & Stratton 20kW if:** a 10-year warranty matters more to you than the biggest dealer network, you've confirmed a competent local servicer, and your loads fit under 18 kW on natural gas (or 20 kW on propane). It's the rational-value pick of the class — the [Briggs & Stratton 20kW review](/generators/briggs-stratton-20kw/) walks through the full ownership case.

**Buy the Generac Guardian 22kW if:** you want the industry's safest pair of hands — the unit your installer knows cold, parts that arrive in days, and the biggest owner community for troubleshooting. It's also the right pick if your load math lands at 19–20 kW on natural gas, where its 19.5 kW NG rating (vs Briggs' 18) keeps you inside spec. See the [Generac Guardian 22kW review](/generators/generac-guardian-22kw/) for the full breakdown, and the [Generac 22kW vs 24kW](/posts/generac-22kw-vs-24kw/) comparison if you're on the fence about sizing up.

**Size up instead if:** your loads exceed 20 kW on your fuel. Neither of these is the answer — look at the [Generac 24kW](/generators/generac-guardian-24kw/) or [26kW](/generators/generac-guardian-26kw/), or the [Kohler 26RCAL](/generators/kohler-26rcal/) for a premium 26 kW option.

Whichever brand you choose, price the *project*, not the box: unit-plus-ATS bundles for both models list around ~$5,400–$6,400, and installation (gas line, pad, electrical, permits) typically takes the project to ~$10,000–$15,000. Run your numbers through the [kW size calculator](/tools/kw-size-calculator/) first, then get three itemized quotes — and confirm local service before falling in love with a spec sheet.
