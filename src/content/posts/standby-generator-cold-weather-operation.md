A standby generator earns its keep on the worst night of the year: an ice storm takes down the lines, the temperature is in the teens, and the house needs heat. That is also the night a generator is hardest to start. Cold thickens engine oil, saps battery cranking power, and punishes every weak link in the starting system at once. Winter operation is therefore less about heroic engineering and more about preparation — the right kit, the right oil, a healthy battery, and a clear path for air and exhaust.

The good news is that none of this is exotic. Manufacturers sell matched cold weather kits, the maintenance is ordinary, and most winter failures are preventable with work done in autumn, in daylight, before anything is urgent. This guide covers what actually goes wrong in the cold, what the kits do, how oil and fuel choices change in winter, and how to keep the unit clear and exercised through the season.

## Why cold weather is hard on a standby generator

Three things happen at once when the temperature drops, and together they explain most winter no-starts.

**The battery loses cranking power.** A lead-acid battery's chemical reactions slow down as it gets colder, so the same battery delivers noticeably less current at 0°F than at 70°F. The starter motor, meanwhile, needs *more* current than usual, because it has to turn an engine full of cold, thick oil. That widening gap — less available power, more power demanded — is why a battery that cranked fine in October can fail in January. Battery warmers exist precisely to narrow it, and Generac recommends its battery heater kit whenever temperatures regularly fall below 32°F (0°C).

**The oil thickens.** Engine oil is rated by how it flows at temperature. A grade that pours freely in summer can turn sluggish in a deep freeze, delaying lubrication to bearings and valve gear in the first seconds after startup — the seconds when wear happens. Switching to the winter grade your manual specifies, usually a synthetic 5W grade, keeps oil flowing to the parts that need it. This is also why extreme cold weather kits from Generac specify replacing the oil with the appropriate synthetic oil at installation: the heater helps, but the oil still has to flow.

**Everything else gets stiffer and slower.** Seals contract, fuel regulators respond more slowly, and any moisture in the crankcase breather system can freeze. Air-cooled Generac models have a specific version of this problem: the crankcase breather can ice up in extreme cold, which is why Generac's 7103 cold kit for 9–22 kW air-cooled units addresses breather icing alongside battery and oil heating. You do not need to understand the mechanism in detail — you need to know that the kit exists, that it is model-specific, and that your installer or dealer should confirm the right part number for your unit.

None of this means standby generators are unreliable in winter. It means a unit installed for a mild climate and never adapted for a northern winter is being asked to do a job it was not set up for. The fixes below are the adaptation.

## Cold weather kits: battery warmers, oil warmers, and block heaters

Cold weather kits are not one product. They are a family of thermostat-controlled heaters, and knowing which one does what keeps you from buying the wrong half of the solution.

**Battery warmers (battery heater pads or blankets).** A pad or wrap that holds the battery near its working temperature. Generac's 7211 battery heater kit, for 9,000–28,000 watt air-cooled models, wraps the battery and uses an integrated thermostat that switches on at 40°F and off at 55°F, so it only draws power when it is actually cold. That thermostat matters: a heater running all winter in a mild spell wastes electricity and can shorten battery life, while a heater you have to remember to switch on will be off on the night you need it. Automatic, thermostat-controlled warming is the standard to look for, whatever brand you buy.

**Oil warmers.** These heat the oil directly, usually through a slip-ring element in the oil filter adapter path on Generac's 7101 kit for 9–22 kW air-cooled units. Warm oil flows immediately at startup, which cuts starter strain and prevents the dry-start wear that cold, thick oil causes. The 7101 kit combines the oil warmer with a thermal battery wrap and activates automatically below a set threshold, and it plugs into a standard 120V outlet — which means it needs a dedicated outlet at or near the generator enclosure. If your pad has no power nearby, that outlet is part of the install, not an afterthought.

**Engine block heaters.** On larger liquid-cooled units, the heater warms the engine coolant and, through it, the block itself. Generac's extreme cold weather kits pair a block heater with a battery heater, and the two are meant to be used together: the block heater alone leaves a cold battery trying to crank a warm engine, and the battery heater alone leaves warm cranking power fighting cold oil. For liquid-cooled models the block heater may be a 120V or 240V unit drawing 1,500–2,000 watts depending on the kit, and Generac recommends dealer installation for these kits, since incorrect installation can impair generator function. Budget for the electrical circuit, too — some kits require a 20-amp circuit from the house panel.

**Which kit fits your generator?** Kits are narrowly model-specific. Generac's 7103 fits 9–22 kW air-cooled units; the 5616 extreme cold kit fits 22–60 kW liquid-cooled models; the 5630 battery warmer fits select 2.4L liquid-cooled units. Kohler, Cummins, and Briggs & Stratton each sell their own cold weather accessories with their own fit lists. The rule is simple and non-negotiable: confirm the kit part number against your exact model and serial range before ordering, and have a dealer or installer fit it if the instructions call for one. A kit for the wrong model is not a bargain — it is a heater that does not fit, does not heat the right surface, or both.

As a rough guide to cost, cold weather kits for residential air-cooled units typically run in the ~$100–$300 range before installation, with larger liquid-cooled kits higher; check current prices, since accessory pricing moves and bundles vary. The heater's running cost is modest — a thermostat-controlled pad or oil warmer draws power only in cold weather — but it does need that outlet, and in an outage the heater is irrelevant: it does its work *before* the outage, keeping the unit ready.

## Oil viscosity: the cheapest winter upgrade

If you do one thing before winter, change the oil to the grade your manual specifies for cold weather. It costs little, needs no electrician, and directly addresses the thick-oil problem.

The pattern across brands is consistent, even though the exact grades differ by model. Kohler guidance commonly cited for its engines calls for 5W-20 or 5W-30 below 30°F and 10W-30 above 30°F, with synthetic oil preferred for easier cold starts. Generac manuals similarly call for 5W-30 or 10W-30 synthetic in many air-cooled models, with the exact recommendation depending on the expected temperature range. The first number — the "W" number — is the cold-flow rating: lower means the oil flows more readily when cold. The second number is the hot viscosity. A 5W-30 synthetic therefore starts like a thin oil and protects like a 30-weight oil once warm, which is exactly what a standby engine needs when it goes from stone cold to full load in seconds.

Two practical points. First, change the oil *before* the cold arrives, and check the level at the same time — standby engines hold little oil, and low oil triggers an automatic shutdown that looks exactly like a winter failure. Second, do not improvise grades. A heavier oil "because winter is hard on engines" is backwards: heavier oil is harder to pump when cold. If your manual lists a range, pick the grade for the coldest temperature your area realistically sees, and ask your dealer if the manual is ambiguous for your model. ## Propane vs natural gas in extreme cold

Fuel choice matters in winter, but not in the simple way it is sometimes sold. Both fuels work in the cold. They fail differently, and knowing the failure mode tells you what to watch.

**Propane: easy starting, tank-dependent supply.** Propane is stored as a liquid and drawn off as vapor, and it vaporizes readily even in very cold weather — propane's boiling point is far below any temperature a house will see. That is why propane generators are often described as easier cold starters: there is no choke, no stale fuel, and no carburetor to gum up. The catch is the *rate* of vaporization. A tank supplies vapor from its liquid surface, and that rate falls as the tank gets colder and as the liquid level drops. A large, well-filled tank — the 250- or 500-gallon tanks common for standby service — has plenty of surface area and thermal mass. A small tank, or a big tank run down to a quarter full during a multi-day cold snap, can struggle to feed a 20-plus kW generator at full load even though the tank is not empty. The fix is unglamorous: keep the tank filled before winter, size the tank for the generator as well as the house, and ask your propane supplier to check vaporization capacity for your coldest expected temperature and your generator's full-load draw. The [propane tank sizing guide](/posts/propane-tank-size-for-standby-generator/) works through the runtime math, and the [fuel consumption guide](/posts/standby-generator-fuel-consumption/) shows how quickly a tank empties under load.

**Natural gas: no tank, but derated and utility-dependent.** A natural gas generator draws from the utility line, so there is no tank to fill, no vaporization limit, and no delivery truck to wait for in a storm. That is a real advantage in a long outage. The trade-off is output: the same generator is typically rated lower on natural gas than on propane, because natural gas carries less energy per cubic foot — roughly 1,000–1,030 BTU per cubic foot against propane's roughly 2,500 BTU per cubic foot in vapor terms. A unit marketed as 24 kW on propane, such as the [Generac Guardian 24 kW](/generators/generac-guardian-24kw/), is commonly rated around 21 kW on natural gas, and the [Kohler 20RESCL](/generators/kohler-20rescl/) is rated 20 kW on propane and 18 kW on natural gas. Size from the rating on *your* fuel, not the headline number — the [sizing guide](/posts/standby-generator-sizing-guide/) and the [kW size calculator](/tools/kw-size-calculator/) both make this explicit. Natural gas also depends on the utility staying pressurized, which it usually does in winter storms, but gas service can be interrupted by earthquakes, excavation damage, or utility shutoffs in fire-risk conditions. In most of the country, in most winters, it is the lower-maintenance winter fuel.

**The regulator and the line.** Both fuels pass through a regulator, and regulators can ice up or respond sluggishly in extreme cold, particularly if moisture is present. This is installer territory, not a homeowner adjustment: if a generator runs rough or surges only in very cold weather, a regulator or fuel-pressure issue belongs on the service call list, alongside the battery and oil checks. Do not adjust fuel pressure yourself — incorrect pressure can damage the engine or create a safety hazard.

For most buyers the decision is already made by what is available at the address. If you have a choice, weigh propane's independence and full rating against natural gas's freedom from tank management. The full comparison, including costs and runtime, is in the [natural gas vs propane guide](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-generator/).

## Snow, ice, and clearance: keeping the unit breathing

A standby generator is an air-breathing machine. It needs combustion air in, cooling air through, and exhaust out — and snow is remarkably good at blocking all three at once.

**Clear the enclosure after every snowfall.** Brush snow off the top and sides, and clear a path around the unit so the intake and exhaust openings are unobstructed. Drifting is the enemy: a unit on the lee side of the house can be buried on one side while the driveway looks clear. Check the unit the way you check the furnace vent — as part of the storm routine, not as an afterthought when the power is already out. Do not pile shoveled snow against the enclosure, and do not let a snowblower throw snow into the intake side.

**Mind the exhaust and the roofline.** Exhaust must go away from the house and away from windows, doors, and vents — the same 5-foot opening clearance that governs placement year-round. In winter, add two checks: icicles and snow sliding off a roof above the unit can block the top vents or damage the enclosure, and exhaust in still, cold air can linger near the ground rather than dispersing. If your unit sits under a roof valley or eave, talk to your installer about whether drifting snow or ice fall is a realistic risk for that spot, and never enclose the unit in a box, tarp, or improvised shelter — restricting airflow will overheat the engine or trap exhaust, and it voids the installation clearances the unit was listed under.

**Ice on the pad and access.** The pad should stay level and drained; ice buildup around the base can block the bottom air path on some enclosures and makes service access miserable. Keep the route a technician would take to the unit walkable in winter — the service visit you need in January should not require a shovel expedition first. If you use a de-icing product near the pad, keep it off the enclosure and the electrical components.

**Do not cover the generator.** Breathable manufacturer covers exist for some models for storage, but a running standby generator must never be covered, and a cover left on over an exercise cycle can overheat the unit. If you use a cover between storms, remove it before the unit is expected to run, and confirm with your dealer that the cover is approved for your model.

## Exercise cycles in winter: let them run, and listen

The weekly exercise cycle is the generator's self-test: it starts, runs unloaded or lightly loaded for several minutes, and shuts down. In winter it does double duty — it proves the battery, oil, and fuel system can still start the engine in the cold, and it circulates oil and charges the battery. Let it run all winter, on its normal schedule.

**Do not disable the exercise cycle because it is cold.** Some owners worry that short winter runs are hard on the engine. The opposite is true: a generator that sits untouched from November to a February outage is the one that fails. The exercise cycle keeps seals lubricated, keeps the battery charged through the onboard charger, and surfaces problems — a slow crank, a fault code, a low-battery warning — while there is still time to fix them. If your controller offers an exercise mode that runs the engine at reduced speed, that quieter mode is fine for the weekly test; the full-speed capability is proven during longer runs and during actual outages.

**Schedule the exercise for a sensible time, and keep it consistent.** Mid-morning on a weekday is the least disruptive slot for neighbors and the easiest time for you to notice a problem — a unit that exercises at 2 a.m. in January will be heard, and a unit that exercises while you are at work will not be. Many controllers let you set the day and time; pick one, write it down, and check that the unit actually ran. A missed exercise cycle is information: it may mean a dead battery, a tripped breaker on the charger circuit, or a fault that needs a service call.

**Listen and look once a month.** During or just after an exercise run, walk out and check for anything new: unusual cranking slowness, warning lights or fault codes on the controller, exhaust discoloration, ice on the enclosure, or a battery warning. You do not need to diagnose anything — you need to notice a change early and call it in. The controller's fault history is worth checking after any failed start, and the battery deserves a specific look: most standby batteries last roughly 3–5 years, and a battery entering its fourth winter is a candidate for proactive replacement before the coldest month, not after it fails. If the unit has a battery warmer, confirm the heater is plugged in and its outlet has power — a warmer on a tripped GFCI is doing nothing.

## A winter readiness checklist

Work through this in autumn, and again after the first hard freeze:

- **Battery:** Test or have the battery tested; replace it if it is old, slow to crank, or showing a low-voltage warning. Confirm the battery warmer is installed, plugged in, and powered.
- **Kit:** Confirm the correct cold weather kit for your exact model is installed — battery warmer, oil warmer or block heater as applicable — and that any required outlet or circuit is live.
- **Oil:** Change to the winter synthetic grade your manual specifies, and check the level. Note the grade and date for next year.
- **Fuel:** Fill the propane tank before winter and confirm the supplier's delivery schedule; on natural gas, confirm the meter and regulator are accessible and undamaged.
- **Clearance:** Clear vegetation, leaves, and debris from around the enclosure; confirm the 5-foot opening clearance and service access are still met after any landscaping changes.
- **Snow plan:** Decide who clears the unit after a storm, and keep the path to it clear. Do not stack snow against the enclosure.
- **Exercise:** Confirm the weekly exercise is scheduled, note the day and time, and verify the last few cycles actually ran.
- **Controller:** Check for fault codes or warnings, and make sure you know how to read the battery and maintenance alerts your model shows.
- **Dealer number:** Keep your installer's or dealer's service number somewhere you can find it in an outage — and know that service demand spikes during a storm, so pre-winter maintenance beats an emergency call.

Winter is when a standby generator either justifies itself or embarrasses its owner. The difference is almost never the brand on the enclosure. It is whether the unit was set up for the climate it lives in, whether the battery and oil were ready, and whether anyone noticed the warning signs while they were still small. Do the autumn work, let the exercise cycle do its weekly reporting, and the coldest night of the year becomes the night the system was built for.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do standby generators need a cold weather kit?

In climates where temperatures regularly fall below freezing, yes — or at least a battery warmer. Manufacturers recommend cold weather kits below 32°F (0°C), and a cold battery plus thick oil is the most common winter no-start combination. In mild climates with rare hard freezes, the standard setup is usually enough.

### What oil should I use in a standby generator in winter?

Use the winter grade your owner's manual specifies — commonly a synthetic 5W-20 or 5W-30 below 30°F, versus 10W-30 above it for some Kohler engines. Change it before winter arrives, check the level, and do not substitute a heavier grade: heavier oil flows worse when cold.

### Will a standby generator start at 0°F or below?

It can, when properly equipped — battery warmer, oil or block heater, correct synthetic oil, and a healthy battery — but a bare unit in those conditions is a gamble. Check your model's published operating range in its manual, and treat a slow crank during an exercise cycle as an early warning, not a quirk.

### Is propane or natural gas better for a standby generator in cold weather?

Propane starts readily and stores indefinitely, but a small or low tank can struggle to vaporize fast enough for a large generator in extreme cold. Natural gas has no tank to manage but is usually derated a few kilowatts. Either works with the right setup; size from the rating on your fuel and keep propane tanks filled before winter.
