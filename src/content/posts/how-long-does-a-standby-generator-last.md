It's the question every standby buyer asks, usually right after the price: how long will this thing actually last? The honest answer comes in two clocks — **engine hours** and **calendar years** — and the one that runs out first is rarely the one people expect.

The short version: a maintained air-cooled home standby generator typically lasts **15–30 years**. The long version involves a ~3,000-hour design life, a weekly exercise cycle that barely dents it, and a list of age-related failure modes that kill more generators than worn-out engines ever will.

## The industry numbers: ~3,000 hours air-cooled, 10,000–30,000 liquid-cooled

Installer and industry sources commonly use **~3,000 running hours** as the design-life planning figure for air-cooled home standby engines. Liquid-cooled standby units — larger engines, tighter temperature control, typically running at 1,800 RPM instead of 3,600 — are commonly estimated at **10,000–30,000 hours**.

Two caveats before you do the math. First, these are *planning numbers*, not manufacturer-published death dates — no brand stamps an expiration hour on the enclosure. Second, they describe the engine. The generator is an engine plus an alternator, a transfer switch, electronics, an enclosure, and a battery, and the engine block is rarely the first thing to fail. Keep both facts in mind as you read on.

## The math: why 15–30 years is normal

Here's why those hour figures turn into decades. A typical home standby in normal backup duty runs:

- **Weekly exercise:** ~20 minutes per week on the factory cycle — about **17–26 hours per year**. (Newer eco/exercise modes can cut this further.)
- **Outages:** the average US customer sees only a handful of outage hours per year; even a brutal year with a multi-day outage adds roughly 72 hours for a 3-day blackout.

Put it together and a normal year is **well under 100 running hours**. Against a ~3,000-hour design life, the engine hour clock runs out somewhere around year 30 — and in practice, almost nothing dies on the hour clock first. If you want the full picture on what running actually costs per hour of fuel, the [standby generator fuel consumption](/posts/standby-generator-fuel-consumption/) guide has real spec-sheet figures.

## The two clocks: engine wear vs. calendar age

Think of your generator as wearing out on two clocks at once:

**The hour clock** measures engine wear — bearings, rings, valves, oil contamination. This is the clock the exercise cycle and outages advance, and it's the clock that liquid-cooled units dominate.

**The age clock** measures everything else — batteries sulfating, rubber fuel lines and seals hardening, electrical connections corroding, enclosures weathering, controllers aging out of parts availability. This clock advances whether the generator runs or not.

The key insight from service techs: **most residential standbys die of age-clock causes with plenty of engine life remaining**. A neglected unit can fail in year 8 with 300 hours on the meter; a pampered one can run past year 25. The age clock is why the annual maintenance floor applies even in zero-outage years — the generator doesn't know it never ran.

## What actually kills standby generators early

Ranked by how often service techs see them:

1. **The battery.** The starter battery is the number-one "my generator didn't start during the outage" cause — by a wide margin. Batteries last **2–3 years**; a maintainer (trickle charger) extends that, but the battery is a maintenance item, not a lifetime part. Replace it on a schedule, not when it fails.
2. **Deferred maintenance.** Oil and filter changes, air filters, spark plugs, valve adjustments — the annual service that owners skip because the unit "ran fine last year." The number-one pattern in dead-at-outage units is years without service.
3. **Disabled exercise cycles.** Owners silence the weekly test because it's noisy or "annoying." Then a seized transfer-switch linkage, a dead battery, or a fouled fuel system hides until the outage reveals it. The [standby generator maintenance](/posts/standby-generator-maintenance/) guide covers what the exercise is actually checking.
4. **Corrosion.** Coastal installs, road-salt spray, and lawn-irrigation overspray all attack enclosures and connections. Aluminum enclosures (Kohler, Generac) resist this better than steel; either way, keep sprinklers and downspouts aimed away from the unit.
5. **Undersizing and overloading.** Running a 14 kW unit at 100% for days stresses everything; proper sizing with margin — see the [standby generator sizing guide](/posts/standby-generator-sizing-guide/) — is a longevity decision, not just a comfort one.
6. **Fuel issues (propane).** Sizing the tank for the runtime you actually want — the [propane tank size guide](/posts/propane-tank-size-for-standby-generator/) covers this — and keeping regulator and line in service condition. Natural gas eliminates this category entirely, which is part of why [natural gas vs propane](/posts/natural-gas-vs-propane-generator/) is a bigger decision than buyers expect.

Notice what's missing from the list: worn-out engines. On the hour clock, a maintained residential standby almost never reaches its design limit before the age clock or a parts-availability problem ends the story.

## How to actually reach 25+ years

The owners who get three decades out of a standby do the same boring things:

- **Annual professional service.** Oil and filter change yearly (or per the manual's hour interval, whichever comes first — the first oil change on a new unit is typically at ~25–30 hours of break-in, then yearly or every 200 hours). Air filter, spark plugs, and valve lash per the schedule. This is the single highest-ROI spend in standby ownership.
- **Battery on a schedule.** Replace every 2–3 years; keep it on a maintainer between services. Write the install date on the battery with a paint pen so you know where you stand.
- **Exercise cycle stays enabled.** Twenty minutes a week, and confirm it actually ran — free remote monitoring (Generac Mobile Link, Briggs InfoHub, Kohler OnCue) will show you.
- **Keep it clear.** Three to five feet of clearance around the enclosure, snow and leaves brushed off the intake/exhaust before and during winter outages, nothing stored against it.
- **Annual visual check.** Rodent nesting (mice love warm enclosures), chewed wiring, cracked rubber fuel lines, corroded battery terminals, and standing water near the pad. Five minutes with a flashlight.
- **Surge protection on the ATS.** A whole-home surge protector at the transfer switch protects the generator's controller and your home's electronics alike — cheap insurance for the circuit board that decides whether the machine starts.
- **Don't ignore faults.** A fault code that clears itself is still a fault. Log it, look it up, and call service if it repeats.

## Does brand change the lifespan?

Honestly: less than the brochure implies. What differentiates brands on lifespan is mostly **(a)** warranty length as a proxy for how confident the manufacturer is, and **(b)** parts availability over decades.

On warranties: the [warranty comparison](/posts/standby-generator-warranty-comparison/) shows the spread — Briggs & Stratton's PowerProtect line leads at 10-year limited parts, Generac/Kohler/Cummins at 5 years (Generac with no hour cap, Kohler and Cummins at 2,000 hours). A longer warranty doesn't make the machine last longer, but it signals the manufacturer expects it to survive.

On parts: Generac's scale advantage becomes a lifespan advantage late in life. A 20-year-old Guardian still has parts available because millions of them exist; a 20-year-old unit from a small line may be parts-obsolete at 12. This is the quiet argument for the [Generac Guardian 22kW](/generators/generac-guardian-22kw/) and [24kW](/generators/generac-guardian-24kw/) — and one reason service techs will tell you the most common unit is the easiest unit to keep alive forever.

On engines: Vanguard (Briggs) commercial pedigree, G-Force (Generac) purpose-built standby, Kohler's commercial Power Systems lineage, Cummins' industrial diesel heritage — all fine. The maintenance log matters more than the engine badge.

## The liquid-cooled question

Should you buy a liquid-cooled unit for longevity? The numbers are seductive — 10,000–30,000 hours sounds like forever. And for the right buyer it is the right machine: homes over ~30–40 kW of managed load, properties with frequent long outages, or buyers who want 1,800 RPM quiet and multi-decade engine margins.

But the honest math for typical homes: a maintained air-cooled 20–26 kW unit already delivers 15–30 years of backup service, and liquid-cooled units cost roughly **double or more** than comparable air-cooled capacity. The extra engine hours are insurance on a risk you don't have. Buy liquid-cooled when your loads require the capacity or you want the operational benefits (quieter, lower RPM, longer service intervals) — not to extend an already-adequate lifespan.

## Repair or replace at year 15–20

Eventually the age clock wins. Around years 15–20, have a licensed technician run the repair-vs-replace math with you. The rules of thumb:

- A mid-four-figure repair on a unit with a healthy engine, documented service history, and available parts is usually worth doing.
- An engine rebuild or controller replacement on a 20-year-old unit with obsolete electronics usually isn't — the new unit brings a fresh 5–10 year warranty and modern monitoring.
- **Parts availability often decides before the engine does.** Get a replacement quote alongside any major repair estimate, and weight the remaining expected life honestly.

And when you do replace, the [installation cost](/posts/standby-generator-installation-cost/) guide still applies — the pad, gas line, and much of the electrical work may be reusable, which can meaningfully cut the second project's price.

## The bottom line

A standby generator is a 20–30-year appliance *if* you treat the age clock with the same respect as the hour clock: annual service, battery on schedule, exercise enabled, unit kept clear, faults taken seriously. The engine will outlast the machine around it — your job is making sure the machine around it survives long enough for that to matter. Buy right ([sizing guide](/posts/standby-generator-sizing-guide/)), maintain yearly ([maintenance guide](/posts/standby-generator-maintenance/)), and the generator you install this decade will still be standing guard in the next one.
